\begin{abstract}
The Platonic Representation Hypothesis posits that foundation models converge toward a shared representation of reality, but does not characterize its geometry. We show that the inter-class distance structure of class concepts learned by vision models, causal language models, and text embedding models is hierarchical, specifically tree-like, consistent with hyperbolic geometry. Its negative curvature and exponential volume growth make it a natural host to tree-like structure. Hence, this representation can be exploited more efficiently in hyperbolic space than in Euclidean space, when the right downstream conditions are met. Using Gromov $\delta$-hyperbolicity and Ollivier--Ricci curvature (ORC) as hyperbolicity metrics on 30 models (14 vision, 9 causal language models (LMs), and 7 text embeddings), we show that it is the training objective, and not the modality, that determines the geometry. Self-supervised vision models and causal LMs learn the most hierarchical representations. Contrastive models, vision and text alike, learn moderately hierarchical ones, and the strongest within-family scaling appears in self-supervised vision ($\delta$ drops by 45\% from DINOv2-S (22M parameters) to DINOv2-G (1.1B)). Controlled ablations confirm that this hierarchy is learned rather than architectural: it disappears in random-weight models, weakens with non-hierarchical fine-tuning, and emerges progressively across transformer layers. Beyond description, across 10 vision backbones and 6 datasets, a parameter-free post-hoc Poincar\'{e} projection of frozen features improves prototype-based classification and few-shot learning, with the magnitude of the gain predictable from a single backbone-level diagnostic and conditioned on the dataset having a real label hierarchy. On flat-hierarchy datasets, no advantage emerges, a negative result is consistent with the underlying geometric mechanism. Hierarchical, tree-like geometry is therefore both a universal property of learned representations and a zero-cost decision tool for situations when hyperbolic distances help downstream.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
\paragraph{Capacity meets data.} The capacity of the model (parameters, depth, training objective) controls how rich a structure it can encode, and the intrinsic hierarchy of the data controls what is worth encoding. Larger models have more expressive room; data with a deep semantic hierarchy fills that room with a richer organisation. Empirically, both ingredients are required: a small model on hierarchical data hits a capacity wall, and any model on flat data has nothing hierarchical to learn. This suggests the geometry that emerges is neither a property of the architecture alone nor of the dataset alone, it is a co-product of the two, and should scale with both.


\paragraph{The Platonic Representation Hypothesis (PRH) and hierarchical reality.} The PRH of \citet{huh2024platonic} provides evidence that, as foundation models scale, they converge toward a shared representation of reality. The PRH measures how they converge, but leaves the geometry of the shared representation unspecified. Reality, as humans organise it, is fundamentally hierarchical: instances belong to categories (a husky is a dog), categories to superordinate classes (dogs are mammals), and superordinate classes to broader concepts (mammals are animals). The natural mathematical home for such hierarchies is the hyperbolic geometry~\citep{nickel2017poincare, sarkar2011low}. It accommodates exponentially growing branching factors with low distortion, whereas Euclidean space fails to embed even modestly deep trees without distortion. If foundation models really do converge to a representation of a hierarchical reality, that representation should bear a hyperbolic signature, and the magnitude of that signature should depend on both the model's capacity and the dataset's intrinsic hierarchy.



\paragraph{But, does the platonic representation have hyperbolic geometry, and how does it scale with capacity and data hierarchy?} When we look at the inter-class distance matrix produced by a frozen foundation model, is it tree-like in the metric sense, and, if so, can we exploit that structure downstream without any retraining? Figure~\ref{fig:intro_concept} frames the underlying question: an observer of reality only sees projections of an unknown structure; we ask whether that structure is hierarchical, in which case the natural geometry is hyperbolic, or flat, in which case standard Euclidean distances suffice. Prior hyperbolic-deep-learning work mostly imposes hyperbolic structure through specialized architectures, losses, or trainable Poincar\'{e} embeddings~\citep{nickel2017poincare, Khrulkov_2020_CVPR, chen2022fully, desai2023meru}. We take the opposite path: we never train anything new. Instead, we measure how hierarchical (tree-like) the natural geometry already is using two complementary, zero-cost diagnostics, Gromov $\delta$-hyperbolicity~\citep{Gromov1987} and Ollivier--Ricci curvature (ORC)~\citep{ollivier2009ricci}, computed directly on raw Euclidean centroids. We then test the prediction that the more tree-like a model's centroid geometry, the more hyperbolic potential it carries, both as an embedding and as a downstream decision rule.

\textbf{Our contributions are:}
\begin{enumerate}[topsep=0pt,itemsep=1pt,leftmargin=*]
\item {We show that \textbf{the hierarchical (tree-like) geometry of a learned representation is a co-product of model capacity and data hierarchy}. Across 30 models and 6 datasets, the strength of the hierarchy scales with parameter count most clearly within self-supervised and supervised vision, while contrastive families (vision and text alike) are essentially flat in both descriptors; across datasets, deeper-hierarchy ones (e.g.\ ImageNet) yield lower $\delta$ than flat-hierarchy ones (e.g.\ MNIST). It is the training objective, not the modality, that determines how tree-like the learned geometry is.
\item \textbf{Causal evidence that the geometry is learned}: the hierarchy disappears with random weights, weakens with non-hierarchical fine-tuning, emerges progressively across transformer layers, and is preserved under shuffle controls.
\item $\delta$ \textbf{and ORC correlate with the hyperbolic advantage}, in prototype-based classification and few-shot learning across 10 vision backbones $\times$ 6 datasets ($n = 60$). Low $\delta$ and high ORC, computed once on the backbone's centroids, predict the gain a Poincar\'{e} projection delivers downstream.
\item \textbf{$\mathbb{H}$ vs. $\mathbb{R}$}: given a backbone and dataset, the two zero-cost diagnostics tell when post-hoc hyperbolic projection pays off and when it does not.
\end{enumerate}



\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}


\paragraph{Hyperbolic deep learning across modalities.} A growing line of work imposes hyperbolic geometry by design as a hierarchy-friendly inductive bias, building on \citet{nickel2017poincare}'s Poincar\'{e} embeddings of taxonomies, anchored in \citet{sarkar2011low}'s tree-embedding result, and \citet{ganea2018hyperbolic}'s differentiable hyperbolic layers. The idea has branched across different modalities. In computer vision, hyperbolic classifiers for CNNs~\citep{Khrulkov_2020_CVPR}, ViTs~\citep{ermolov2022hyperbolic}, dense segmentation~\citep{atigh2022hyperbolic}, and fully hyperbolic CNNs~\citep{bdeir2024fully}. In vision--language, MERU~\citep{desai2023meru} and the recent HyCoCLIP~\citep{pal2025compositional} trained entirely in $\mathbb{H}$. In graphs, one can find the hyperbolic graph convolutional networks~\citep{chami2019hyperbolic}. In language, billion-parameter fully hyperbolic LLMs~\citep{he2026helm}, fully hyperbolic transformers~\citep{chen2022fully}, and hyperbolic regularisation injecting label hierarchy into supervised features~\citep{sinha2024learning}. The closest precedent to our paper is HypLoRA~\citep{yang2025hyperbolic}, which observes that token embeddings of standard Euclidean LLMs already exhibit a degree of latent hyperbolicity and exploits it through fine-tuning on the hyperbolic manifold. We take the opposite path: we never train, fine-tune, or regularise the model. Instead, we make the latent-hyperbolicity observation systematic across 30 foundation models spanning vision, causal language, and text embeddings. We characterise the geometry with two coordinate-free descriptors, isolate its causal origin in training rather than architecture, and translate it into a parameter-free downstream decision rule.

\paragraph{Geometric analyses of learned representations.} A separate line studies the properties of representations without imposing curvature: linear probing accuracy as a quality proxy, representation similarity via centered kernel alignment~\citep{kornblith2019cka}, intrinsic dimension of penultimate features~\citep{ansuini2019intrinsic, pope2021intrinsic}, and the equiangular geometry of class means in the terminal training phase known as neural collapse~\citep{papyan2020prevalence}. These analyses operate on Euclidean point clouds and do not test for tree-like or hyperbolic structure. The closest precedents to ours are \citet{Khrulkov_2020_CVPR} and \citet{bdeir2024fully}, who report low $\delta$ on a handful of CNNs/ViTs but stop short of (i)~comparing across paradigms or modalities, (ii)~establishing that the geometry is causally produced by training (rather than architecture), and (iii)~translating the descriptor into a downstream decision rule. We address these three gaps explicitly and at scale.


\paragraph{Hyperbolic space and trees.} Hyperbolic geometry is the natural geometric 'home' for hierarchical (tree-like) data. Volumes of hyperbolic balls grow exponentially with radius, while Euclidean balls in $\mathbb{R}^n$ grow only polynomially. The consequence is sharp: \citet{sarkar2011low} shows that arbitrary trees embed into the two-dimensional Poincar\'{e} disk $\mathbb{H}^2$ with arbitrarily low distortion, whereas embedding even modestly deep trees in $\mathbb{R}^n$ with bounded distortion provably requires the dimension to grow with the depth of the tree. The standard realisations of hyperbolic space, the Poincar\'{e} ball, the Lorentz (hyperboloid) model, and the upper half-space, are mutually isometric models of the constant negative-curvature space, all admitting closed-form distance functions. The post-hoc evaluation in this paper uses the Poincar\'{e}-ball distance directly, without any learnable parameters. \citet{Gromov1987}'s $\delta$-hyperbolicity formalises how tree-like a metric space is in a coordinate-free way, requiring neither an embedding nor a curvature value. % and is the descriptor we use throughout.



\paragraph{Platonic Representation Hypothesis and convergence analyses.} The PRH~\citep{huh2024platonic} argues, via kernel alignment and kNN overlap, that vision and language models converge toward a shared representation as they scale. Our characterization is a within-model property that stands on its own: even if the cross-model alignment evidence is weakened by spectral or dataset-side confounds, the geometry of each foundation model's inter-class distances remains hierarchical, learned, and predictive of downstream behavior. We add a geometric dimension to the PRH that does not require cross-model comparison to be meaningful.



\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}
%% ============================================================

Our methodology is based on two parts: a set of descriptive tools that quantify how tree-like a frozen representation is (\S\ref{sec:descriptors}), and a prescriptive tool that turns those measurements into a downstream decision rule (\S\ref{sec:projection}--\S\ref{sec:downstream}). Both operate on raw Euclidean centroids and require no retraining or learnable parameters.

\subsection{Embeddings and centroids}
\label{sec:embeddings}

We treat each foundation model as a fixed feature extractor and obtain a single vector per model and sample pair in one forward pass. Vision backbones produce the CLS token at the output of the final transformer block; causal language models produce the last-token hidden state; text-embedding models use their native sentence-transformer pooling. To measure inter-class structure rather than per-image variability, we collapse each class to a single centroid: vision centroids are the per-class mean of training embeddings, and for language and text-embedding models we encode the prompt ``a photo of a \{class\}''.

\subsection{Two coordinate-free descriptors of tree-likeness}
\label{sec:descriptors}

$\delta$-hyperbolicity and ORC both operate directly on the centroid distance matrix and are invariant to the embedding dimension and basis. They capture complementary aspects of tree-like structure: $\delta$ is a global property of the inter-centroid metric, while ORC is a local, edge-wise notion of curvature.

\textbf{Gromov $\delta$-hyperbolicity.} A metric space is $\delta$-hyperbolic in the sense of \citet{Gromov1987} if it satisfies a four-point condition. For any four class centroids $x, y, z, w \in X$ (with $X$ the set of $C$ class centroids in $\mathbb{R}^d$ produced by the model), ordering the three pairwise sums $S_1{=}d(x,y){+}d(z,w)$, $S_2{=}d(x,z){+}d(y,w)$, $S_3{=}d(x,w){+}d(y,z)$ as $S_{\max} \ge S_{\text{mid}} \ge S_{\min}$, the four-point defect is $(S_{\max}-S_{\text{mid}})/2$, and
\begin{equation}
\delta = \max_{x,y,z,w} \frac{S_{\max} - S_{\text{mid}}}{2}.
\end{equation}
Trees achieve $\delta = 0$ \citep{Gromov1987}; the unit hyperbolic plane has $\delta = \log(1{+}\sqrt{2})$ \citep{Gromov1987}; Euclidean spaces of dimension $\geq 2$ have unbounded $\delta$ \citep{Gromov1987}. We normalise $\delta_{\text{norm}} = \delta / \mathrm{diam}(X)$, where $X$ denotes the set of class centroids, for cross-model comparability and use ``low $\delta$'' as shorthand for ``tree-like''. We approximate the maximum by sampling $500{,}000$ random quadruples per seed and averaging over $10$ seeds; as sanity checks the estimator recovers the analytical $\delta = 0.000$ on a binary tree and $\delta = 0.127$ on the unit sphere $S^{99} \subset \mathbb{R}^{100}$ (the $99$-dimensional sphere), and the estimate is invariant under isometries (variation $<10^{-4}$ across seeds).

\textbf{Ollivier--Ricci curvature.} On the $k{=}10$ kNN graph over centroids, we compute ORC~\citep{ollivier2009ricci} per edge as
\begin{equation}
\kappa(u,v) = 1 - \frac{W_1(\mu_u, \mu_v)}{d(u, v)},
\end{equation}
where $\mu_u$ and $\mu_v$ are the uniform distributions on the kNN neighbours of $u$ and $v$, respectively, and $W_1$ is the $1$-Wasserstein distance. Following \citet{ollivier2009ricci}, negative-curvature edges concentrate around branch points of the tree-like skeleton, so the fraction of negative edges and the mean ORC give a local, edge-level view that complements the global $\delta$.

\subsection{Post-hoc Poincar\'{e} projection (parameter-free)}
\label{sec:projection}

To turn the geometric measurements into a downstream decision rule, we map any frozen Euclidean feature into the unit Poincar\'{e} ball $\mathbb{B}^d$ (constant negative curvature $-1$)~\citep{nickel2017poincare, ganea2018hyperbolic} without training a single parameter. The procedure is determined entirely by training-set statistics:
\begin{enumerate}[topsep=0pt,itemsep=1pt,leftmargin=*]
\item Compute the mean $\boldsymbol\mu$ of the training features and the 95th-percentile centred norm $\rho_{95} = \mathrm{quantile}_{95}(\|\boldsymbol{x} - \boldsymbol\mu\|)$;
\item Set the scale $s = 2\,\mathrm{arctanh}(1/\sqrt{2})/\rho_{95}$ so the 95th-percentile feature lands at norm $1/\sqrt{2}$ inside the unit ball;
\item Apply the exponential map at the origin,
\begin{equation}
\phi(\boldsymbol{x}) = \tanh\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\|s(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)\|\Big) \cdot \frac{s(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)}{\|s(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)\|}.
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
The target $1/\sqrt{2}$ keeps the bulk of the feature cloud firmly in the hyperbolic regime. At this radius the Poincar\'{e} conformal factor $\lambda(\boldsymbol{x}) = 2/(1-\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^2)$ already takes the amplified value $\lambda(1/\sqrt{2})^2 = 4\,\lambda(\mathbf{0})^2$. At the same time, $1/\sqrt{2}$ stays away from the boundary saturation regime~\citep{guo2022clipped} where $\lambda \to \infty$, gradients vanish, and Poincar\'{e} distances are dominated by the radial coordinate alone. Distances between projected points use the canonical Poincar\'{e} formula
\begin{equation}
d_\mathbb{B}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}) = \mathrm{arcosh}\Big(1 + \frac{2\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}\|^2}{(1-\|\boldsymbol{u}\|^2)(1-\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^2)}\Big).
\end{equation}
The Euclidean and hyperbolic versions of every downstream task cost identical compute on the same features; the only thing that changes between them is the metric.

\subsection{Downstream protocol}
\label{sec:downstream}

For each model and dataset pair, we evaluate two parameter-free, prototype-based metric tasks, each in two versions: Euclidean (R) on the raw feature vectors $\boldsymbol{x}$, and hyperbolic (H) on their post-hoc Poincar\'{e} projection $\phi(\boldsymbol{x})$ from \S\ref{sec:projection}.
\begin{itemize}[topsep=0pt,itemsep=1pt,leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{NC (Nearest-Centroid).} Per-class training mean as the prototype; classify each test sample by argmin distance to prototypes.
\item \textbf{FS (Few-shot 5-way 5-shot).} $1{,}000$ episodes per model and dataset; per episode $5$ classes, $5$ support + $15$ query samples; classify queries by nearest support-prototype, in the spirit of Prototypical Networks~\citep{snell2017proto}.
\end{itemize}
For each task, we report the hyperbolic advantage
\begin{equation}
H_\text{adv} = (\mathrm{acc}_H - \mathrm{acc}_R) \times 100 \;\; \text{(percentage points)},
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{acc}_R$ denotes the accuracy under raw Euclidean distances and $\mathrm{acc}_H$ the accuracy after applying the post-hoc Poincar\'{e} projection $\phi$ of \S\ref{sec:projection}; both are measured on the same task and split.
Note that $H_{adv}$ is positive when the Poincar\'{e} projection beats the raw Euclidean baseline and negative otherwise. All protocols are training-free: no trainable heads, no learnable distances, no hyperparameter search. Any difference between $R$ and $H$ is attributable to the geometry alone.


\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Models.} We used 30 foundation models in three paradigms: 14 vision backbones (4 supervised ViTs, 4 self-supervised, 6 contrastive), 9 causal language models, and 7 text-embedding models. We work with two settings: a main panel of 10 vision backbones evaluated for $\delta$, ORC and two downstream tasks across all 6 datasets (the supervised ViT-T/S/B/L pretrained on ImageNet-21k~\citep{dosovitskiy2021an}, the self-supervised DINOv1-B~\citep{dinov1} and DINOv2-S/B/L/G~\citep{dinov2}, and the contrastive CLIP-B/L~\citep{CLIP}); and a breadth panel of 20 additional models evaluated for cross-modality $\delta$ on ImageNet only (the contrastive variants OpenCLIP~\citep{openclip} and MetaCLIP~\citep{metaclip}; the causal language models GPT-2~\citep{gpt2}, Pythia~\citep{pythia} and OLMo~\citep{olmo}; and the text-embedding families BGE~\citep{BGE}, GTE~\citep{gte} and E5~\citep{e5}).


\textbf{Datasets and evaluation.} For the cross-model $\delta$ and ORC characterisation (\S\ref{sec:geometry-paradigm}) we use the $1{,}000$ classes of ImageNet~\citep{imagenet} as a shared concept set: vision backbones produce one centroid per class by averaging features of $100$ training images, while causal language models and text-embedding models encode the prompt ``a photo of a \{class\}'' once per class. This common $1{,}000 \times d$ matrix lets us compare $\delta$ and ORC across modalities on equal footing. For the downstream protocol (\S\ref{sec:downstream-results}) we use the $10$ vision backbones of the main panel $\times$ $6$ datasets ($n = 60$): ImageNet~\citep{imagenet}, CIFAR-100~\citep{cifar10}, CIFAR-10~\citep{cifar10}, DTD~\citep{dtd}, FashionMNIST~\citep{fashion}, and MNIST~\citep{mnist}.


\subsection{Per model and dataset results}
\label{sec:complete-results}

Three patterns emerge from our per model and dataset measurements of $\delta$, ORC, R/H accuracies and $H_{\mathrm{adv}}$ across both NC and FS tasks. (1)~For DINOv2-L/G on hierarchical datasets (ImageNet, CIFAR-100, CIFAR-10, DTD), $H_{\mathrm{adv}}$ is positive on both NC and FS. (2)~For supervised and contrastive backbones, $H_{\mathrm{adv}}$ on NC is mostly negative while FS still benefits modestly. (3)~On flat-hierarchy datasets (FashionMNIST, MNIST), all advantages collapse toward zero.

\subsection{Geometry across capacity and paradigm}
\label{sec:geometry-paradigm}

Across all 30 models we find a striking pattern (Figure~\ref{fig:scaling}, Table~\ref{tab:within-family}): self-supervised vision (DINOv2) and large causal LMs (GPT-2~XL) achieve the lowest $\delta$ and the strongest within-family scaling, while vision contrastive and text-embedding families cluster around $\delta \approx 0.11$--$0.12$ with negligible scaling. The training objective, not the modality, determines the geometry.

Causal-LM families reach $\delta$ values comparable to the most tree-like vision models ($\delta \approx 0.08$ at scale) and ORC up to $+0.41$, despite never seeing an image. Text embedders are flatter and operate at lower ORC ($\sim$$0.20$), matching the geometry of vision contrastive backbones in (b) and reinforcing that the training objective, not the modality, controls hierarchy.}

\subsection{Downstream task results}
\label{sec:downstream-results}

We evaluate the post-hoc Poincar\'{e} projection of \S\ref{sec:projection} on the two tasks of \S\ref{sec:downstream}, across $10$ vision models $\times$ $6$ datasets ($n = 60$).


\textbf{Task NC.} The largest gains concentrate where the geometry can be most exploited, the most tree-like backbones on the deepest-hierarchy datasets. DINOv2-L gains $\mathbf{+2.10}$ pp on CIFAR-100 (a $2.5\%$ relative improvement on a $85.6\%$ baseline) and $+0.80$ pp on DTD; DINOv2-G gains $+1.20$ pp on CIFAR-100 and DINOv2-S $+1.00$ pp, lower $\delta$ predicts higher $H_{\mathrm{adv}}$ as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:synthesis_pooled}. Within-dataset correlations are strongest on hierarchical datasets, reaching $r = -0.89$ on ImageNet and $r = -0.70$ on CIFAR-100; flat-hierarchy datasets (FashionMNIST, MNIST) show no significant correlation. ImageNet NC gains are themselves smaller in absolute terms ($+0.30$ pp for DINOv2-L).

\textbf{Task FS.} The pooled correlation is $r = -0.36$, weaker than for NC because, at this curvature, the few-shot projection helps almost every model and dataset pair ($54/60$ positive $H_{\mathrm{adv}}$, mean $+0.54$ pp), compressing the variance against which $\delta$ can be tested. Within the hierarchical datasets the dependence on $\delta$ is again strong: $r = -0.84$ on CIFAR-100, $r = -0.81$ on ImageNet, $r = -0.70$ on CIFAR-10. All $10$ vision models on ImageNet gain in few-shot ($H_{\mathrm{adv}}$ from $0.00$ to $+1.10$ pp), with the largest gains again in the SSL family (DINOv2-G $+1.10$, DINOv2-L $+0.90$, DINOv2-B $+0.70$).

\textbf{Practical protocol.} Compute $\delta$ on training-set class centroids (${\sim}8$~s on CPU). If $\delta < 0.10$ and the labels carry a deep semantic hierarchy, the post-hoc Poincar\'{e} projection of frozen features improves NC and few-shot accuracy by $0.5$--$2.5$~pp; otherwise, retain Euclidean. The decision is reversible (a one-line distance switch), requires no training, and is unconfounded by optimisation tuning, making the geometry a deployable diagnostic rather than only a descriptive one.

\subsection{Causal ablations: $\delta$ reflects learned hierarchy, not architectural noise}
\label{sec:ablation-noise}

To distinguish architectural bias and label noise from learned hierarchy, we run four interventions on representative vision models. Each isolates a single causal factor as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:causal}.

\textbf{(A1) Random weights.} Random-initialised models have dramatically higher $\delta$: DINOv2-B ($+144\%$), DINOv2-S ($+134\%$), ViT-B ($+65\%$). Tree-like structure is not architectural; it emerges through training.

\textbf{(A2) Non-hierarchical fine-tuning.} Fine-tuning on an 8-class color classification task (no class hierarchy by construction) increases $\delta$: CLIP-B ($+91\%$), DINOv2-S ($+69\%$), ViT-B ($+19\%$). Fine-tuning on hierarchical CIFAR-100 produces minimal change ($-8\%$ to $+6\%$). Same protocol, different task structure, only destroying hierarchy raises $\delta$.

\textbf{(A3) Layer-wise emergence.} Computing $\delta$ on class centroids (1{,}000 ImageNet classes, $50$ imgs/class) at each transformer block, we observe that $\delta$ decreases progressively from layer~1 to the final layer in pretrained transformers: DINOv2-B ($-51\%$, from $0.157$ to $0.077$) and CLIP-B ($-29\%$, from $0.157$ to $0.112$). Hierarchy is not present in shallow layers but is built up across depth, confirming that tree-like structure is a learned property of the representation rather than an artefact of token-level features.

\textbf{(A4) Shuffle control (label noise).} Shuffling embedding-to-label correspondence preserves the point-cloud geometry but destroys semantic association: $\delta$ does not change ($p > 0.12$). This rules out a trivial dependence on high-dimensional point-cloud properties and confirms that $\delta$ measures semantic organisation, not feature norm or covariance.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:reproducibility}

All models and datasets used in this paper are publicly available; the post-hoc Poincar\'{e} projection of \S\ref{sec:projection} is a single \texttt{torch}-level operation determined entirely by training-set statistics ($\boldsymbol\mu$ and $\rho_{95}$). Computing $\delta$ on $1{,}000$ class centroids takes ${\sim}8$~s on CPU, and the full downstream evaluation across the $10$ vision backbones $\times$ $6$ datasets ($n = 60$ combinations) runs in $<\!30$~minutes on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%% ============================================================



We characterised the geometry of platonic representations and found tree-like inter-class distance structure as a universal property across modalities and paradigms. Across 30 models, two coordinate-free descriptors ($\delta$ and ORC) converge on the same conclusion. Causal ablations establish that this geometry reflects learned semantic hierarchy: random weights, non-hierarchical fine-tuning, and layer-wise analysis all support the link. The discovery that causal LMs are also tree-like overturns the assumption that this property is vision-specific. The tree structure is visualised explicitly in Figure~\ref{fig:tree_visualization} for DINOv2-S and DINOv2-G on CIFAR-100.

The geometry is not merely descriptive, but prescriptive. Backbone $\delta$ and ORC predict when post-hoc Poincar\'{e} projection improves prototype-based classification (pooled $r = -0.46$ vs $\delta$, $r = +0.32$ vs ORC; up to $r = -0.89$ within ImageNet) and few-shot learning (pooled $r = -0.36$ vs $\delta$, $r = +0.44$ vs ORC; up to $r = -0.84$ within CIFAR-100), and when they do not (datasets without label hierarchy). Computing both descriptors takes seconds on CPU, providing a zero-cost diagnostic that tells practitioners whether to use hyperbolic geometry on their specific backbone, dataset combination, and when not to.

The two findings close into a single arc: within hierarchy-respecting paradigms (most strongly self-supervised vision: DINOv2 drops $\delta$ by $45\%$ from S to G while ORC rises from $+0.35$ to $+0.73$), scaling up the backbone makes the geometry simultaneously more tree-like and more curved, and our correlations show that this is exactly the regime where the post-hoc hyperbolic projection delivers its largest gains (e.g.\ DINOv2-L on CIFAR-100: $H_{\mathrm{adv}}^{\text{NC}} = +2.10$~pp). Capacity makes the platonic representation tree-like, and tree-likeness, in turn, makes it exploitable in $\mathbb{H}$.

\textbf{Limitations.} The downstream evaluation covers only two prototype-based tasks, nearest-centroid classification and 5-way 5-shot, and on the headline target $t = 1/\sqrt{2}$ the hyperbolic advantage at any single model and dataset combination is modest in absolute terms ($H_{\mathrm{adv}} \approx 0.5$--$2.5$~pp); whether the same diagnostic predicts gains on retrieval, dense prediction, or end-to-end training in $\mathbb{H}$ is not tested here. The Poincar\'{e} ball is fixed at constant negative curvature $-1$ throughout by design, to keep the projection parameter-free; ablating curvature would reintroduce a hyperparameter and is left as future work.
