\begin{abstract}
The Platonic Representation Hypothesis posits that foundation models converge toward a shared representation of reality, but does not characterize its geometry. We show that the inter-class distance structure of vision models, causal language models, and text embedding models is hierarchical, specifically tree-like, consistent with hyperbolic geometry, meaning that this representation can be exploited more efficiently in hyperbolic space than in Euclidean space, when the right downstream conditions are met. Using Gromov $\delta$-hyperbolicity and Ollivier--Ricci curvature (ORC) as hyperbolicity metrics on 39 models (22 vision, 9 causal LMs, 8 text embeddings), we find that the training objective, not the modality, determines the geometry: self-supervised vision models and causal LMs learn the most hierarchical representations, contrastive models, vision and text alike, learn moderately hierarchical ones, and within each family hierarchy strengthens monotonically with scale. Controlled ablations confirm that this hierarchy is \emph{learned} rather than architectural: it disappears in random-weight models, weakens with non-hierarchical fine-tuning, and emerges progressively across transformer layers. Crucially, the geometry is also prescriptive. Across 12 vision backbones and 6 datasets, a parameter-free post-hoc Poincar\'{e} projection of frozen features improves prototype-based classification and few-shot learning, with the magnitude of the gain predictable from a single backbone-level diagnostic and conditioned on the dataset having a real label hierarchy; on flat-hierarchy datasets and on symmetric retrieval, no advantage emerges, a negative result consistent with the underlying geometric mechanism. Hierarchical, tree-like geometry is therefore both a universal property of learned representations and a zero-cost decision tool for when hyperbolic distances help downstream and when they do not.
\end{abstract}

\begin{abstract}
La Platonic Representation Hypothesis postula que los foundation models convergen hacia una representación compartida de la realidad, pero no caracteriza su geometría. Mostramos que la estructura de distancias inter-clase de modelos de visión, modelos causales de lenguaje y modelos de embeddings de texto es \emph{jerárquica}---específicamente \emph{tree-like}, consistente con geometría hiperbólica---lo que significa que esa representación puede explotarse más eficientemente en el espacio hiperbólico que en el euclídeo cuando se dan las condiciones downstream adecuadas. Usando $\delta$-hyperbolicity y Ollivier--Ricci curvature sobre 39 modelos (22 de visión, 9 LMs causales, 8 de embeddings de texto), encontramos que el \emph{objetivo} de entrenamiento, no la modalidad, determina la geometría: los modelos SSL de visión y los LMs causales aprenden las representaciones más jerárquicas, los modelos contrastivos---visión y texto---aprenden jerarquías moderadas, y dentro de cada familia la jerarquía se intensifica monotónicamente con la escala. Ablaciones controladas confirman que esta jerarquía es \emph{aprendida} y no arquitectónica: desaparece en modelos con pesos aleatorios, se debilita con fine-tuning no jerárquico, y emerge progresivamente a través de las capas del transformer. Por último, la geometría también es \emph{prescriptiva}. A través de 12 backbones de visión y 6 datasets, una proyección Poincar\'{e} post-hoc sin parámetros aplicada a features congeladas mejora la clasificación basada en prototipos y el few-shot learning, con magnitudes de ganancia predecibles a partir de un único diagnóstico al nivel del backbone y condicionadas a que el dataset tenga una jerarquía real de etiquetas; en datasets sin jerarquía y en retrieval simétrico, no aparece ventaja---un resultado negativo consistente con el mecanismo geométrico subyacente. La geometría jerárquica tree-like es por lo tanto tanto una propiedad universal de las representaciones aprendidas como una herramienta de decisión de coste cero para saber cuándo las distancias hiperbólicas ayudan downstream y cuándo no.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%% ============================================================

\paragraph{Motivation.} The geometry of learned representations governs which downstream operations are well-conditioned: nearest-neighbor retrieval, classification, clustering, and metric-based alignment all rely on inter-point distances behaving like the structure of the underlying data. Yet most of what we know about foundation-model representations focuses on \emph{linear} properties (rank, alignment, kernel similarity, probing accuracy) and treats the embedding space as Euclidean by default. Whether this default is correct for what models actually learn is, surprisingly, an open question.

\paragraph{The Platonic Representation Hypothesis (PRH).} The PRH of \citet{huh2024platonic} sharpens this question by providing evidence that foundation models converge toward a shared representation of reality as they scale: kernel-alignment and kNN-overlap statistics rise with model size across vision and language. The PRH measures \emph{that} models converge, but it leaves the geometry of the shared representation unspecified. Two foundational observations make this gap matter. First, the structure of reality is fundamentally \emph{hierarchical}: instances belong to categories (a husky is a dog), categories to superordinate classes (dogs are mammals), and superordinate classes to broader concepts (mammals are animals). Second, the natural geometry for hierarchies is \emph{hyperbolic}~\citep{nickel2017poincare, sarkar2011low}: hyperbolic space accommodates exponentially growing branching factors with low distortion, whereas Euclidean space provably cannot embed even modestly deep trees without distortion. If foundation models really do converge to a representation of a hierarchical reality, that representation should bear a hyperbolic signature---and we should be able to measure it directly, on the standard Euclidean embeddings these models already produce.

\paragraph{The question this paper asks.} \emph{Does the platonic representation have hyperbolic geometry?} Concretely: when we look at the inter-class distance matrix produced by a frozen foundation model, is it tree-like in the metric sense---and, if so, can we exploit that structure downstream without any retraining? Figure~\ref{fig:intro_concept} illustrates the central intuition: a perfect binary hierarchy (panel a) suffers irreducible distortion when forced into a low-dimensional Euclidean space (panel b)---branches collapse, distant cousins look as close as siblings---whereas the same hierarchy is preserved by an embedding into the Poincar\'{e} disk (panel c), where the exponential growth of hyperbolic volume accommodates the branching. Prior hyperbolic-deep-learning work mostly \emph{imposes} hyperbolic structure through specialized architectures, losses, or trainable Poincar\'{e} embeddings~\citep{nickel2017poincare, khrulkov2020hyperbolic, chen2022fully, desai2023meru}. We take the opposite path: we never train anything new. Instead, we measure how hierarchical (tree-like) the natural geometry already is using Gromov $\delta$-hyperbolicity~\citep{gromov1987hyperbolic} and Ollivier--Ricci curvature (ORC)~\citep{ollivier2009ricci}, two zero-cost statistics that operate directly on raw Euclidean centroids, and ask under what conditions a parameter-free post-hoc projection into the Poincar\'{e} ball pays off in standard downstream tasks.

(b)~Embedded in $\mathbb{R}^2$ via classical MDS, the tree's structure is irreducibly distorted: distinct branches are pulled into close proximity and the radial separation between superordinate categories vanishes. (c)~Embedded in the Poincar\'{e} disk ($\mathbb{H}^2$) by Riemannian optimization, the same tree is preserved: the exponential growth of hyperbolic volume near the boundary accommodates branching with low distortion. \paragraph{Scope of the study.} We evaluate 39 models across five paradigms---22 vision (4 supervised ViTs, 9 self-supervised, 9 contrastive, 1 contrastive-hyperbolic), 9 causal language models, and 8 text embedding models---all on the same 1{,}000 ImageNet classes for direct comparability. We then run controlled ablations on representative models (random-weight reset, non-hierarchical fine-tuning, layer-wise emergence, shuffle controls) to isolate the causal role of training. Finally, we evaluate a parameter-free Poincar\'{e} projection on 12 vision backbones $\times$ 6 datasets ($n = 72$) across three downstream tasks (nearest-centroid classification, 5-way 5-shot few-shot learning, and sample-to-sample retrieval) to test whether $\delta$ is not only descriptive but \emph{prescriptive}: a zero-cost diagnostic of \emph{when} hyperbolic distances help downstream and when they do not.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[topsep=0pt,itemsep=1pt,leftmargin=*]
\item Foundation-model representations have a \textbf{universal hierarchical (tree-like) inter-class structure} that is \textbf{more efficiently exploitable in hyperbolic than in Euclidean space}. The training \emph{objective}, not the modality, determines the degree and type of hierarchy that emerges.
\item \textbf{Causal language models are tree-like too}, contrary to the common assumption that this property is vision-specific. Their hierarchies match those of self-supervised vision models, while contrastive text and contrastive vision models cluster together at moderate hierarchy.
\item \textbf{Causal evidence} that this geometry is \emph{learned} rather than architectural: it disappears with random weights, weakens with non-hierarchical fine-tuning, emerges progressively across transformer layers, and is preserved under shuffle controls.
\item $\delta$ \textbf{and} ORC are \textbf{practical, zero-cost decision tools} for post-hoc hyperbolic projection. The hyperbolic advantage in prototype-based classification and few-shot learning is predictable from these two backbone-level diagnostics alone, and the conditions under which it disappears (symmetric retrieval, datasets without label hierarchy) are explainable from the same geometric mechanism.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%% ============================================================

\paragraph{Hyperbolic geometry in deep representations: imposed vs.\ emergent.} A growing line of work imposes hyperbolic structure through architectures or losses. \citet{nickel2017poincare} embed taxonomies into the Poincar\'{e} ball using Riemannian SGD, exploiting the exponential volume growth of $\mathbb{H}^n$ to fit hierarchies with low distortion in few dimensions; \citet{sarkar2011low} provides the underlying combinatorial result that arbitrary trees can be embedded in $\mathbb{H}^2$ with arbitrarily low distortion. \citet{khrulkov2020hyperbolic} replace the final Euclidean layer of CNNs with a Poincar\'{e} hyperplane and report uniformly low $\delta$ across three CNNs; \citet{chen2022fully} introduce fully hyperbolic neural networks operating natively on the Lorentz model; \citet{desai2023meru} (MERU) train a CLIP-style image--text model entirely in hyperbolic space. Common to all of these is that hyperbolic geometry is built in by design. Our work is complementary: we measure how hierarchical (tree-like) the natural geometry already is in standard Euclidean architectures, and ask whether it can be exploited \emph{post-hoc}, with no retraining and no architectural change. Where MERU and Poincar\'{e} embeddings answer ``does imposing $\mathbb{H}$ help?'', we answer ``has $\mathbb{H}$ already emerged, and if so, when does it pay off?''---two questions that have been mostly disjoint in the literature.

\paragraph{Geometric analyses of learned representations.} A separate line studies properties of representations without imposing curvature: linear probing accuracy~\citep{alain2017probes}, representation similarity via centered kernel alignment~\citep{kornblith2019cka}, intrinsic dimension of penultimate features~\citep{ansuini2019intrinsic, pope2021intrinsic}, and the equiangular geometry of class means in the terminal training phase known as neural collapse~\citep{papyan2020prevalence}. These analyses operate on Euclidean point clouds and do not test for tree-like or hyperbolic structure. The closest precedents to ours are \citet{khrulkov2020hyperbolic} and \citet{bdeir2024fully}\footnote{Reference details verified for camera-ready.}, who report low $\delta$ on a handful of CNNs/ViTs but stop short of (i)~comparing across paradigms or modalities, (ii)~establishing that the geometry is causally produced by training (rather than architecture), and (iii)~translating the descriptor into a downstream decision rule. We address these three gaps explicitly and at scale.

\paragraph{Platonic Representation Hypothesis and convergence analyses.} The PRH~\citep{huh2024platonic} argues, via kernel alignment and kNN overlap, that vision and language models converge toward a shared representation as they scale. Concurrent work~\citep{groger2026revisiting} questions parts of the convergence evidence by isolating spectral and dataset-side confounds. Our characterization is an independently interesting \emph{within-model} property: even if the cross-model alignment evidence is weakened, the geometry of \emph{each} foundation model's inter-class distances remains hierarchical, learned, and predictive of downstream behavior. We add a geometric dimension to the PRH that does not require cross-model comparison to be meaningful.

\paragraph{Foundation models studied.} Vision: supervised ViTs~\citep{dosovitskiy2021vit}; self-supervised DINOv1/v2~\citep{caron2021dino, oquab2024dinov2}, BarlowTwins~\citep{zbontar2021barlow}, VICReg~\citep{bardes2022vicreg}, BYOL~\citep{grill2020byol}; contrastive CLIP~\citep{radford2021clip}, SigLIP~\citep{zhai2023siglip}, OpenCLIP~\citep{cherti2023openclip}, MetaCLIP~\citep{xu2024metaclip}, MERU~\citep{desai2023meru}. Causal LMs: GPT-2~\citep{radford2019gpt2}, Pythia~\citep{biderman2023pythia}, OLMo~\citep{groeneveld2024olmo}. Text embeddings: BGE~\citep{xiao2024bge}, E5~\citep{wang2024e5}, GTE~\citep{li2023gte}, Nomic. Datasets: ImageNet~\citep{russakovsky2015imagenet}, ImageNet-Sketch~\citep{wang2019sketch}, Places-365~\citep{zhou2018places}, CIFAR~\citep{krizhevsky2009cifar}, DTD~\citep{cimpoi2014dtd}, FashionMNIST, MNIST.

\paragraph{Prototype-based classification, few-shot, and post-hoc evaluation.} Our downstream protocol uses three parameter-free recipes that are all instances of metric-based classification on frozen features. Nearest-centroid classification dates back to template-matching baselines and is the natural endpoint of representation evaluation when no head is allowed. Few-shot prototypical networks~\citep{snell2017proto} popularised the same prototype-then-distance idea in the meta-learning regime, and a long line of follow-up work shows that \emph{frozen} pretrained backbones combined with simple prototype distances are surprisingly competitive on standard meta-learning benchmarks. Sample-to-sample retrieval against a labeled corpus is the prototypical (no pun intended) zero-cost evaluation in image search. We adopt all three deliberately: each preserves the geometry of the backbone's features (no learnable encoder, no MLP head), so any difference between Euclidean and Poincar\'{e} versions is attributable to the distance function alone. This isolates the geometric question---can hyperbolic distances exploit a tree-like centroid structure?---from confounds with optimisation, capacity, or hyperparameter search that would arise with learned classifiers.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}
%% ============================================================

\paragraph{Metric: Gromov $\delta$-hyperbolicity.} A metric space is $\delta$-hyperbolic~\citep{gromov1987hyperbolic} if for any four points $x, y, z, w$, ordering the three pairwise sums $S_1{=}d(x,y){+}d(z,w)$, $S_2{=}d(x,z){+}d(y,w)$, $S_3{=}d(x,w){+}d(y,z)$ as $S_{\max} \ge S_{\text{mid}} \ge S_{\min}$,
\begin{equation}
\delta = \max_{x,y,z,w} \frac{S_{\max} - S_{\text{mid}}}{2}.
\end{equation}
Trees have $\delta = 0$; the unit hyperbolic plane has $\delta = \log(1{+}\sqrt{2})$; Euclidean spaces have unbounded $\delta$. We normalise $\delta_{\text{norm}} = \delta / \mathrm{diam}(X)$ for cross-model comparability and use ``hyperbolic'' as shorthand for ``low $\delta$.''

\paragraph{Embedding extraction.} Vision: CLS token of the final layer. Causal LMs: last-token hidden state. Text-embedding models: native sentence-transformer pooling. All in a single forward pass.

\paragraph{Centroids.} For vision we average embeddings of 50 images per class, yielding 1{,}000 ImageNet centroids. For text/LM we encode the prompt ``a photo of a \{class\}''. This measures \emph{inter-class} distance structure.

\paragraph{Sampling.} 500{,}000 random quadruples $\times$ 10 seeds; we report mean $\delta_{\text{norm}}$ and standard deviation. Sanity checks: a binary tree gives $\delta = 0.000$; the sphere $S^{99}$ gives $\delta = 0.127$; the metric is invariant under isometries ($< 10^{-4}$).

\paragraph{Auxiliary geometric measures.} (i)~\textbf{Ollivier--Ricci curvature (ORC)}~\citep{ollivier2009ricci} on the $k{=}10$ kNN graph, computed via 1-Wasserstein distance between neighbourhood measures; (ii)~\textbf{four-point defects}: distribution of $S_{\max}-S_{\text{mid}}$ over 100K quadruples, compared against synthetic references ($\mathbb{R}^{10}$, $\mathbb{H}^{10}$, $S^{10}$); (iii)~\textbf{three-way distortion}: embed centroids into $\mathbb{R}^{10}$ (Adam-optimised stress, MDS), $\mathbb{H}^{10}$ (Riemannian Adam on the Poincar\'{e} ball, search $c \in \{0.5, 1, 2\}$), and discrete trees (best of UPGMA, Ward, MST), reporting mean relative distortion. Implementation details in Appendix.

\paragraph{Post-hoc Poincar\'{e} projection (S4 pipeline).} For practical evaluations we apply a parameter-free pipeline to frozen backbone features: \textbf{(1)} compute training mean $\boldsymbol\mu$ and 95th-percentile centred norm $\rho_{95} = \mathrm{quantile}_{95}(\|\boldsymbol{x} - \boldsymbol\mu\|)$; \textbf{(2)} choose scale $s = 2\,\mathrm{arctanh}(0.5)/\rho_{95}$; \textbf{(3)} apply the exponential map at the origin,
\begin{equation}
\phi(\boldsymbol{x}) = \tanh\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\|s(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)\|\Big) \cdot \frac{s(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)}{\|s(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)\|}.
\end{equation}
This places the 95th-percentile feature norm at 0.5 inside the unit Poincar\'{e} ball, avoiding shell collapse (where features pile up against the boundary and Poincar\'{e} distance degenerates to a function of angles only). Distances between projected points use the canonical Poincar\'{e} formula, $d_\mathbb{H}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}) = \mathrm{acosh}\!\big(1 + 2\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}\|^2 \big/ \big[(1-\|\boldsymbol{u}\|^2)(1-\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^2)\big]\big)$.

\paragraph{Practical evaluation pipeline.} Three downstream tasks on 12 vision models $\times$ 6 datasets ($n = 72$). All use frozen backbones and the S4 projection above.
\begin{itemize}[topsep=0pt,itemsep=1pt,leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{NC (Nearest-Centroid):} per-class training mean as the prototype; classify each test sample by argmin distance to prototypes.
\item \textbf{FS (Few-shot 5-way 5-shot):} 1{,}000 episodes per (model, dataset); per episode 5 classes, 5 support + 15 query; classify queries by nearest support-prototype~\citep{snell2017proto}.
\item \textbf{R (Sample-to-sample retrieval P@10):} for each test query, retrieve the 10 nearest training samples and measure fraction sharing the query's class (fine) and superclass (sup). 1{,}000 queries per (model, dataset), 50 train samples per class.
\end{itemize}
For each task we compute $H_\text{adv} = (\mathrm{acc}_H - \mathrm{acc}_R) \times 100$ pp.

\section{Ablation Study: $\delta$ reflects learned hierarchy}
\label{sec:ablation}
%% ============================================================

To distinguish architectural bias from learned structure, we run four interventions on representative vision models. Each isolates a single causal factor.

\paragraph{(A1) Random weights.} Random-initialised models have dramatically higher $\delta$: DINOv2-B ($+144\%$), DINOv2-S ($+134\%$), ViT-B ($+65\%$). Tree-like structure is not architectural; it emerges through training.

\paragraph{(A2) Non-hierarchical fine-tuning.} Fine-tuning on an 8-class color classification task (no class hierarchy by construction) increases $\delta$: CLIP-B ($+91\%$), DINOv2-S ($+69\%$), ViT-B ($+19\%$). Fine-tuning on hierarchical CIFAR-100 produces minimal change ($-8\%$ to $+6\%$). Same protocol, different task structure---only destroying hierarchy raises $\delta$.

\paragraph{(A3) Layer-wise emergence.} $\delta$ decreases progressively from layer~1 to the final layer in pretrained transformers: DINOv2-B ($-60\%$), CLIP-B ($-46\%$). A randomly initialised ViT-B shows flat $\delta \approx 0.195$ throughout, ruling out architectural artefacts and confirming that hierarchy is built up across depth.

\paragraph{(A4) Shuffle control.} Shuffling embedding-to-label correspondence preserves the point-cloud geometry but destroys semantic association: $\delta$ does not change ($p > 0.12$). This rules out a trivial dependence on high-dimensional point-cloud properties and confirms that $\delta$ measures \emph{semantic} organisation.

  \centering

  \caption{Ablations confirming that $\delta$ reflects learned hierarchy. (a)~Color (non-hierarchical) fine-tuning increases $\delta$ by 19--91\%. (b)~Shuffling embeddings preserves $\delta$ ($p > 0.12$). (c)~Random-weight models have 65--144\% higher $\delta$ than trained counterparts.}

\paragraph{(A5) Cross-dataset generalization.} The $\delta$ ranking generalises beyond ImageNet: correlations with ImageNet-Sketch ($r = 0.873$, $p < 10^{-6}$) and Places-365 ($r = 0.661$, $p < 0.001$) are highly significant on the 22 vision models. DINOv2 achieves the lowest $\delta$ on all three datasets. The weaker Places-365 correlation reflects its shallower taxonomy (365 scene categories vs.\ 1{,}000 object categories).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{Tree-like geometry is universal}
\label{sec:universal}

Table~\ref{tab:models} reveals a striking pattern across all 39 models. Self-supervised vision models achieve the lowest $\delta$ (DINOv2-L: $0.067$; BarlowTwins: $0.070$), and $\delta$ decreases with scale within families: DINOv2-S to DINOv2-G ($-36\%$), supervised ViT-T to ViT-L ($-25\%$). Contrastive models remain flat ($\delta \approx 0.114$--$0.126$ regardless of scale). Causal LMs achieve comparably low $\delta$ when evaluated on the same 1{,}000 ImageNet classes: GPT-2 XL ($0.073$), OLMo-1B ($0.084$), Pythia-1B ($0.095$). Text embedding models cluster at $\delta \approx 0.114$--$0.124$, identical to vision contrastive models, confirming that the training \emph{objective} (contrastive loss), not the modality, determines the geometry.

Two axes organise the landscape: (1)~\emph{degree} of tree-likeness (SSL/causal $>$ supervised $>$ contrastive) and (2)~\emph{type} of tree---WordNet-aligned vs.\ emergent. Models without explicit text supervision discover their own hierarchy ($\delta$ low, WN $r$ low); models with text alignment learn WordNet's specific hierarchy ($\delta$ moderate, WN $r$ high). Across the 22 vision models, $\delta$ correlates with WordNet alignment ($r = 0.629$, $p = 0.002$).

\paragraph{The $\delta$--WordNet paradox.} DINOv2-L ($\delta = 0.067$, WN $r = 0.214$) is the most tree-like model but has \emph{lower} WordNet correlation than CLIP-B ($\delta = 0.126$, WN $r = 0.387$). This resolves once we distinguish $\delta$ (similarity to \emph{any} tree) from WN $r$ (similarity to \emph{WordNet's} tree). DINOv2 discovers an emergent visual hierarchy---grouping by appearance (motorcycle near bicycle) rather than by WordNet category (motorcycle under ``motor vehicle''). CLIP inherits WordNet's hierarchy from text supervision; its class names derive from WordNet synsets.

\subsection{Geometric characterisation}
\label{sec:geometry}

\paragraph{Three-way embedding distortion.} Across all 40 vision models, $\mathbb{H}^{10}$ outperforms $\mathbb{R}^{10}$ ($\rho(\mathbb{H}/\mathbb{R}) > 1$ in 40/40 cases; range $1.03$--$1.21$). On ImageNet (deep WordNet taxonomy), discrete trees outperform $\mathbb{H}^{10}$ in 17/22 vision models---these representations are so tree-like that even continuous hyperbolic manifolds cannot fully capture them. On Places-365 (365 scene categories, shallow taxonomy), $\mathbb{H}^{10}$ wins 18/22, indicating that the relative advantage of trees vs.\ continuous $\mathbb{H}^{10}$ depends on taxonomic depth.

\paragraph{Four-point defects.} Three regimes emerge from the defect distribution: (i)~\textbf{strongly hyperbolic}---DINOv2 family, GPT-2 XL, OLMo (defect 0.005--0.012, matching concentrated $\mathbb{H}^{10}$); (ii)~\textbf{moderately hyperbolic}---contrastive vision, text embeddings, supervised (defect 0.015--0.024); (iii)~all 40 models far from $\mathbb{R}^{10}$ ($0.037$) and $S^{10}$ ($0.057$).

\paragraph{Ollivier--Ricci curvature.} ORC correlates with $\delta$ across all 40 models ($r = -0.71$, $p < 10^{-4}$). DINOv2 has the highest ORC ($0.52$--$0.65$, 0\% negative edges); supervised models are moderate ($0.31$--$0.38$, 4--7\% negative); contrastive lower ($0.31$--$0.34$, 5--6\%); text embeddings distinctly lower ($0.17$--$0.24$, 13--17\% negative). Ranking is preserved across $k \in \{5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 50\}$ (Spearman $r = 1.0$).

\subsection{Practical payoff: when post-hoc Poincar\'{e} projection helps}
\label{sec:practical}

We evaluate the S4-projection pipeline of Section~\ref{sec:method} on three tasks across 12 vision models $\times$ 6 datasets ($n = 72$).

\paragraph{Task NC.} Pearson correlation between backbone $\delta$ and $H_\text{adv}$ pooled over all 72 combinations: $\boxed{r = -0.542,\;\; p < 10^{-6}}$. Within each individual dataset the correlation is stronger (Table~\ref{tab:within-dataset}), reaching $r = -0.911$ on CIFAR-100 and $r = -0.740$ on ImageNet. DINOv2-L gains $+2.36$ pp on CIFAR-100, $+0.85$ pp on DTD, and $+0.62$ pp on ImageNet.

\paragraph{Task FS.} Pooled $r = -0.496$ ($p < 10^{-5}$). \emph{Every} of the 12 vision models on ImageNet gains in few-shot ($H_\text{adv}$ from $+0.01$ to $+1.31$ pp), and the magnitude scales tightly with $\delta$: within ImageNet $r = -0.868$ ($p = 0.0003$); within DTD $r = -0.919$ ($p < 10^{-4}$).

\paragraph{Task R (sample-to-sample retrieval).} The hyperbolic advantage is uniformly negative or null in every dataset (mean $-0.5$ to $-2.3$ pp), and the correlation with $\delta$ is essentially zero in every dataset (Table~\ref{tab:within-dataset}). This is consistent with the geometric mechanism: when both query and database items are individual samples (both ``noisy''), no asymmetry exists between query and target representation that the radial dimension of the Poincar\'{e} ball can encode. The hyperbolic advantage is therefore restricted to settings where one side is a clean class prototype (NC, FS).

\paragraph{Practical protocol.} Compute $\delta$ on training-set class centroids (${\sim}8$ seconds CPU). \emph{If $\delta < 0.10$ and the labels carry a deep semantic hierarchy}, post-hoc Poincar\'{e} projection of frozen features improves NC and few-shot by $0.5$--$2.5$ pp; \emph{otherwise}, retain Euclidean. The decision is reversible (one-line distance switch), requires no training, and is unconfounded by optimisation tuning.

\paragraph{Full results.} Table~\ref{tab:full-results} reports the complete per-(model, dataset) numerical results: backbone $\delta$ on the dataset's centroids, global ORC, R/H accuracies and $H_{\mathrm{adv}}$ for both NC and FS tasks. Three patterns are visible row by row. (1)~For DINOv2-L/G on hierarchical datasets (ImageNet, CIFAR-100, CIFAR-10, DTD), $H_{\mathrm{adv}}$ is positive on both NC and FS. (2)~For supervised and contrastive backbones, $H_{\mathrm{adv}}$ on NC is mostly negative (Euclidean already aligned with WordNet by design) while FS still benefits modestly. (3)~On flat-hierarchy datasets (FashionMNIST, MNIST), all advantages collapse toward zero. Sample-to-sample retrieval results (column omitted from the table for space) are in \texttt{master\_per\_dataset.csv} and uniformly null---see Section~\ref{sec:practical}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%% ============================================================

We characterised the geometry of platonic representations and found tree-like inter-class distance structure as a universal property across modalities and paradigms. Across 39 models, five geometric metrics ($\delta$, ORC, four-point defects, intrinsic dimension, three-way distortion) converge on the same conclusion. Causal ablations establish that this geometry reflects \emph{learned} semantic hierarchy: random weights, non-hierarchical fine-tuning, and layer-wise analysis all support the link. The discovery that causal LMs are also tree-like overturns the assumption that this property is vision-specific.

The geometry is not merely descriptive but \emph{prescriptive}. Backbone $\delta$ predicts when post-hoc Poincar\'{e} projection improves prototype-based classification ($r = -0.542$ pooled, up to $r = -0.911$ within CIFAR-100) and few-shot learning ($r = -0.496$ pooled, up to $r = -0.919$ within DTD), and when it does not (sample-to-sample retrieval, datasets without label hierarchy). Computing $\delta$ takes seconds on CPU, providing a zero-cost diagnostic that tells practitioners whether to use hyperbolic geometry on their specific backbone--dataset combination---and when not to.

\textbf{Limitations.} $\delta$ measures inter-class centroid geometry; the within-class geometry is partially probed by our few-shot evaluation but not exhaustively. For self-supervised models, low $\delta$ is partly explained by the eigenvalue spectrum (15/22 vision models pass a spectrum-matched null at $p < 0.05$). Causal experiments use $n = 3$--$4$ models on CIFAR-100; extending strengthens the causal claim. Our few-shot evaluation uses random class subsets within each dataset rather than a held-out novel-class split. No model satisfies the strict ultrametric inequality; representations are tree-\emph{like}, not perfect trees.

\textbf{Reproducibility.} All experiments use publicly available models and datasets; the S4 projection pipeline and per-(model, dataset) results are released as scripts and CSVs in the supplementary material. Compute requirements: $\delta$ on 1{,}000 centroids takes 8 s on CPU; the full 72-combination practical evaluation runs in $< 30$ minutes on a single GPU.
