\begin{abstract}
% [COMMITTED] within-model framing promoted into the abstract; "universal"
% softened; matched nulls added; scaling claim restated on the excess basis.
% Everything else is the original abstract.
Foundation models are often studied through cross-model convergence; we characterize instead the geometry each model learns on its own. We show that the inter-class distance structure of class concepts learned by vision models, causal language models, and text embedding models is hierarchical, specifically tree-like, consistent with hyperbolic geometry. Its negative curvature and exponential volume growth make it a natural host for tree-like structure. Hence, this representation can be exploited more efficiently in hyperbolic space than in Euclidean space, when the right downstream conditions are met. Using Gromov $\delta$-hyperbolicity and Ollivier--Ricci curvature (ORC) as hyperbolicity metrics on 30 models (14 vision, 9 causal language models (LMs), and 7 text embeddings), we show that it is the training objective, and not the modality, that determines the geometry. Because random high-dimensional clouds also yield low raw $\delta$, every reading is calibrated against a null matched in dimension and covariance spectrum: 69 of 72 vision model$\times$dataset combinations show tree structure beyond their null. Self-supervised vision models and causal LMs learn the most hierarchical representations. Contrastive models, vision and text alike, learn moderately hierarchical ones, and the strongest within-family scaling appears in self-supervised vision ($\delta$ drops by 33\% from DINOv2-S (22M parameters) to DINOv2-G (1.1B), and the null-corrected excess deepens with scale). % [REGEN] 45->33 with updated ImageNet delta; excess trend added
Controlled ablations confirm that this hierarchy is learned rather than architectural: it disappears in random-weight models, weakens with non-hierarchical fine-tuning, and emerges progressively across transformer layers. Beyond description, across 10 vision backbones and 6 datasets, a parameter-free post-hoc Poincar\'{e} projection of frozen features improves prototype-based classification and few-shot learning, with the magnitude of the gain predictable from a single backbone-level diagnostic and conditioned on the dataset having a real label hierarchy. On flat-hierarchy datasets, no advantage emerges, a negative result that is consistent with the underlying geometric mechanism. Hierarchical, tree-like geometry is therefore a near-universal property of the \emph{form} of learned class representations, model-specific in its content, and a zero-cost decision tool for situations when hyperbolic distances help downstream.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
% [COMMITTED to 1Eqj] first two fragments merged into one paragraph.
\paragraph{Capacity meets hierarchical data.} The capacity of the model (parameters, depth, training objective) controls how rich a structure it can encode, and the intrinsic hierarchy of the data controls what is worth encoding. Larger models have more expressive room; data with a deep semantic hierarchy fills that room with a richer organisation. Reality, as humans organise it, is fundamentally hierarchical: instances belong to categories (a husky is a dog), categories to superordinate classes (dogs are mammals), and superordinate classes to broader concepts (mammals are animals). The natural mathematical home for such hierarchies is hyperbolic geometry~\citep{nickel2017poincare, sarkar2011low}: it accommodates exponentially growing branching factors with low distortion, whereas Euclidean space fails to embed even modestly deep trees without distortion. If a foundation model learns a representation of a hierarchical reality, that representation should bear a tree-like signature, and the magnitude of that signature should depend on both the model's capacity and the dataset's intrinsic hierarchy: a small model on hierarchical data hits a capacity wall, and any model on flat data has nothing hierarchical to learn. The geometry that emerges is neither a property of the architecture alone nor of the dataset alone; it is a co-product of the two, and should scale with both. Cross-model convergence analyses such as the Platonic Representation Hypothesis~\citep{huh2024platonic} ask a complementary question, how similar different models are to one another; we return to the relation between the two questions in Section~\ref{sec:related}. % [COMMITTED] PRH demoted from framing paragraph to a pointer

\paragraph{But, does the learned class representation have tree-like geometry, and how does it scale with capacity and data hierarchy?} When we look at the inter-class distance matrix produced by a frozen foundation model, is it tree-like in the metric sense, and, if so, can we exploit that structure downstream without any retraining? Figure~\ref{fig:intro_concept} frames the underlying question: an observer of reality only sees projections of an unknown structure; we ask whether that structure is hierarchical, in which case the natural geometry is hyperbolic, or flat, in which case standard Euclidean distances suffice. Prior hyperbolic-deep-learning work mostly imposes hyperbolic structure through specialized architectures, losses, or trainable Poincar\'{e} embeddings~\citep{nickel2017poincare, Khrulkov_2020_CVPR, chen2022fully, desai2023meru}. We take the opposite path: we never train anything new. Instead, we measure how hierarchical (tree-like) the natural geometry already is using two complementary, zero-cost diagnostics, Gromov $\delta$-hyperbolicity~\citep{Gromov1987} and Ollivier--Ricci curvature (ORC)~\citep{ollivier2009ricci}, computed directly on raw Euclidean centroids and calibrated against matched random baselines. We then test the prediction that the more tree-like a model's centroid geometry, the more hyperbolic potential it carries, both as an embedding and as a downstream decision rule.

\textbf{Our contributions are:}
\begin{enumerate}[topsep=0pt,itemsep=1pt,leftmargin=*]
\item We show that \textbf{the hierarchical (tree-like) geometry of a learned representation is a co-product of model capacity and data hierarchy}. Across 30 models and 6 datasets, and read against nulls matched in dimension and covariance spectrum (69/72 model$\times$dataset cells show genuine tree structure), the strength of the hierarchy scales with parameter count most clearly within self-supervised and supervised vision, while contrastive families (vision and text alike) are essentially flat in both descriptors; across datasets, deeper-hierarchy ones (e.g.\ ImageNet) yield lower $\delta$ than flat-hierarchy ones (e.g.\ MNIST). It is the training objective, not the modality, that determines how tree-like the learned geometry is. % [COMMITTED] matched nulls added
\item \textbf{Causal evidence that the geometry is learned}: the hierarchy disappears with random weights, weakens with non-hierarchical fine-tuning, emerges progressively across transformer layers, and is preserved under shuffle controls.
\item $\delta$ \textbf{and ORC correlate with the hyperbolic advantage}, in prototype-based classification and few-shot learning across 10 vision backbones $\times$ 6 datasets ($n = 60$). Low $\delta$ and high ORC, computed once on the backbone's centroids, predict the gain a Poincar\'{e} projection delivers downstream.
\item \textbf{$\mathbb{H}$ vs.\ $\mathbb{R}$ (and cosine)}: given a backbone and dataset, the two zero-cost diagnostics tell when a post-hoc hyperbolic projection pays off and when it does not, and matched rescaling and cosine controls locate where the hyperbolic metric itself is the winning choice. % [COMMITTED] three-way protocol
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Hyperbolic deep learning across modalities.} A growing line of work imposes hyperbolic geometry by design as a hierarchy-friendly inductive bias, building on \citet{nickel2017poincare}'s Poincar\'{e} embeddings of taxonomies, anchored in \citet{sarkar2011low}'s tree-embedding result, and \citet{ganea2018hyperbolic}'s differentiable hyperbolic layers. The idea has branched across different modalities. In computer vision, hyperbolic classifiers for CNNs~\citep{Khrulkov_2020_CVPR}, ViTs~\citep{ermolov2022hyperbolic}, dense segmentation~\citep{atigh2022hyperbolic}, and fully hyperbolic CNNs~\citep{bdeir2024fully}. In vision--language, MERU~\citep{desai2023meru} and the recent HyCoCLIP~\citep{pal2025compositional} trained entirely in $\mathbb{H}$. In graphs, one can find the hyperbolic graph convolutional networks~\citep{chami2019hyperbolic}. In language, billion-parameter fully hyperbolic LLMs~\citep{he2026helm}, fully hyperbolic transformers~\citep{chen2022fully}, and hyperbolic regularisation injecting label hierarchy into supervised features~\citep{sinha2024learning}. The closest precedent to our paper is HypLoRA~\citep{yang2025hyperbolic}, which observes that token embeddings of standard Euclidean LLMs already exhibit a degree of latent hyperbolicity and exploits it through fine-tuning on the hyperbolic manifold. We take the opposite path: we never train, fine-tune, or regularise the model. Instead, we make the latent-hyperbolicity observation systematic across 30 foundation models spanning vision, causal language, and text embeddings. We characterise the geometry with two coordinate-free descriptors, isolate its causal origin in training rather than architecture, and translate it into a parameter-free downstream decision rule.

\paragraph{Geometric analyses of learned representations.} A separate line studies the properties of representations without imposing curvature: linear probing accuracy as a quality proxy, representation similarity via centered kernel alignment~\citep{kornblith2019cka}, intrinsic dimension of penultimate features~\citep{ansuini2019intrinsic, pope2021intrinsic}, and the equiangular geometry of class means in the terminal training phase known as neural collapse~\citep{papyan2020prevalence}. These analyses operate on Euclidean point clouds and do not test for tree-like or hyperbolic structure. The closest precedents to ours are \citet{Khrulkov_2020_CVPR} and \citet{bdeir2024fully}, who report low $\delta$ on a handful of CNNs/ViTs but stop short of (i)~comparing across paradigms or modalities, (ii)~establishing that the geometry is causally produced by training (rather than architecture), and (iii)~translating the descriptor into a downstream decision rule. We address these three gaps explicitly and at scale.

% [COMMITTED to 1Eqj] retitled: this subsection is preliminaries, not related work.
\paragraph{Background: hyperbolic space and trees.} Hyperbolic geometry is the natural geometric `home' for hierarchical (tree-like) data. Volumes of hyperbolic balls grow exponentially with radius, while Euclidean balls in $\mathbb{R}^n$ grow only polynomially. The consequence is sharp: \citet{sarkar2011low} shows that arbitrary trees embed into the two-dimensional Poincar\'{e} disk $\mathbb{H}^2$ with arbitrarily low distortion, whereas embedding even modestly deep trees in $\mathbb{R}^n$ with bounded distortion provably requires the dimension to grow with the depth of the tree. The standard realisations of hyperbolic space, the Poincar\'{e} ball, the Lorentz (hyperboloid) model, and the upper half-space, are mutually isometric models of the constant negative-curvature space, all admitting closed-form distance functions. The post-hoc evaluation in this paper uses the Poincar\'{e}-ball distance directly, without any learnable parameters. \citet{Gromov1987}'s $\delta$-hyperbolicity formalises how tree-like a metric space is in a coordinate-free way, requiring neither an embedding nor a curvature value.

% [COMMITTED to RJje/Xbn5] critical PRH literature added; within-model position
% stated with the cross-model measurement.
\paragraph{Platonic Representation Hypothesis and convergence analyses.} The PRH~\citep{huh2024platonic} argues, via kernel alignment and kNN overlap, that vision and language models converge toward a shared representation as they scale. Recent work stress-tests this convergence: \citet{koepke2026cave} examine cross-modal convergence at scale, and \citet{groger2026aristotelian} show that permutation-calibrated similarity removes most apparent global convergence while local neighborhood structure survives; our null-calibration philosophy (Section~\ref{sec:descriptors}) mirrors theirs, applied within models rather than across them. Our characterization is a within-model property that stands on its own: even if the cross-model alignment evidence is weakened by spectral or dataset-side confounds, the geometry of each foundation model's inter-class distances remains hierarchical, learned, and predictive of downstream behavior. Consistently, we find that hyperbolic distances do not increase cross-model agreement in our panel (mutual-kNN alignment $0.474$ Euclidean vs $0.427$ Poincar\'{e} over all 66 model pairs): what is shared across models is the \emph{form} of the class geometry (tree-likeness), not its \emph{content} (each model builds its own tree). % exp5_crossmodel_alignment.csv

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

Our methodology is based on two parts: a set of descriptive tools that quantify how tree-like a frozen representation is (\S\ref{sec:descriptors}), and a prescriptive tool that turns those measurements into a downstream decision rule (\S\ref{sec:projection}--\S\ref{sec:downstream}). Both operate on raw Euclidean centroids and require no retraining or learnable parameters.

\subsection{Embeddings and centroids}
\label{sec:embeddings}

We treat each foundation model as a fixed feature extractor~\citep{dosovitskiy2021an, dinov2, CLIP} and obtain a single vector per model and sample pair in one forward pass. Vision backbones produce the CLS token at the output of the final transformer block; causal language models produce the last-token hidden state; text-embedding models use their native sentence-transformer pooling. To measure inter-class structure rather than per-image variability, we collapse each class to a single centroid: vision centroids are the per-class mean of training embeddings, and for language and text-embedding models we encode the prompt ``a photo of a \{class\}''.

\subsection{Two coordinate-free descriptors of tree-likeness}
\label{sec:descriptors}

$\delta$-hyperbolicity and ORC both operate directly on the centroid distance matrix and are invariant to the embedding dimension and basis. They capture complementary aspects of tree-like structure: $\delta$ is a global property of the inter-centroid metric, while ORC is a local, edge-wise notion of curvature.

\paragraph{Gromov $\delta$-hyperbolicity.} A metric space is $\delta$-hyperbolic in the sense of \citet{Gromov1987} if it satisfies a four-point condition. For any four class centroids $x, y, z, w \in X$ (with $X$ the set of $C$ class centroids in $\mathbb{R}^d$ produced by the model), ordering the three pairwise sums $S_1{=}d(x,y){+}d(z,w)$, $S_2{=}d(x,z){+}d(y,w)$, $S_3{=}d(x,w){+}d(y,z)$ as $S_{\max} \ge S_{\text{mid}} \ge S_{\min}$, the four-point defect is $(S_{\max}-S_{\text{mid}})/2$, and
\begin{equation}
\delta = \max_{x,y,z,w} \frac{S_{\max} - S_{\text{mid}}}{2}.
\end{equation}
% [COMMITTED to 1Eqj] constants corrected: four-point vs thin-triangles definitions,
% absolute vs normalized scales never mixed; calibration under the declared protocol.
Trees achieve $\delta = 0$ \citep{Gromov1987}. Reference constants depend on the definition used: under the four-point condition above, the hyperbolic plane has absolute $\delta = \ln 2 \approx 0.693$ \citep{nica2016strong}, distinct from the thin-triangles constant $\ln(1+\sqrt{2}) \approx 0.881$. We normalise $\delta_{\text{norm}} = \delta / \mathrm{diam}(X)$ for cross-model comparability; on this normalized scale hyperbolic regions drift toward $0$ as they grow while spheres stay high at any radius, so the ordering is the intuitive one: trees $<$ hyperbolic $<$ spherical. We approximate the maximum by sampling $500{,}000$ random quadruples per seed and averaging over $10$ seeds. Table~\ref{tab:calibration} calibrates the exact estimator on reference geometries: it recovers $\delta_{\text{norm}} = 0.000$ on a binary tree, the four-point constant on hyperbolic regions, $0.143$/$0.179$ (chord/geodesic) on the unit sphere $S^{99}$, and, crucially, \emph{low} values on iid Gaussian clouds of growing dimension: random high-dimensional point clouds concentrate toward equidistance, a degenerate star tree. A low raw $\delta_{\text{norm}}$ is therefore not by itself evidence of hierarchy. For every model$\times$dataset cell we consequently also build a null cloud with the same $n$, $d$, mean and covariance spectrum as the real centroids and report the \emph{excess} $\delta_{\text{norm}}^{\text{real}} - \delta_{\text{norm}}^{\text{null}}$ (null s.d.\ $\le 0.007$; a PC-permutation null confirms the readings): throughout, ``tree-like'' means excess below zero, never a low absolute value alone. % exp11_null_per_dataset.csv

\paragraph{Ollivier--Ricci curvature.} On the $k{=}10$ kNN graph over centroids, we compute ORC~\citep{ollivier2009ricci} per edge as
\begin{equation}
\kappa(u,v) = 1 - \frac{W_1(\mu_u, \mu_v)}{d(u, v)},
\end{equation}
where $\mu_u$ and $\mu_v$ are the uniform distributions on the kNN neighbours of $u$ and $v$, respectively, and $W_1$ is the $1$-Wasserstein distance. High mean ORC on the centroid graph reflects tight, overlapping neighbourhoods within semantic clusters, while negative-curvature edges concentrate on the \emph{bridges} between clusters, the tree-like skeleton between branches; the fraction of negative edges and the mean ORC give a local, edge-level view that complements the global $\delta$. % [COMMITTED to RJje/1Eqj] corrected interpretation (bridge analysis, Sec. 4.2)

\subsection{Post-hoc Poincar\'{e} projection (parameter-free)}
\label{sec:projection}

To turn the geometric measurements into a downstream decision rule, we map any frozen Euclidean feature into the unit Poincar\'{e} ball $\mathbb{B}^d$ (constant negative curvature $-1$)~\citep{nickel2017poincare, ganea2018hyperbolic} without training a single parameter. The procedure is determined entirely by training-set statistics (Figure~\ref{fig:expmap}): % [COMMITTED to 1Eqj] diagram added
\begin{enumerate}[topsep=0pt,itemsep=1pt,leftmargin=*]
\item Compute the mean $\boldsymbol\mu$ of the training features and the 95th-percentile centred norm $\rho_{95} = \mathrm{quantile}_{95}(\|\boldsymbol{x} - \boldsymbol\mu\|)$;
\item Set the scale $s = 2\,\mathrm{arctanh}(1/\sqrt{2})/\rho_{95}$ so the 95th-percentile feature lands at norm $1/\sqrt{2}$ inside the unit ball;
\item Apply the exponential map at the origin,
\begin{equation}
\phi(\boldsymbol{x}) = \tanh\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\|s(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)\|\Big) \cdot \frac{s(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)}{\|s(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)\|}.
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
The target $1/\sqrt{2}$ keeps the bulk of the feature cloud firmly in the hyperbolic regime. At this radius the Poincar\'{e} conformal factor $\lambda(\boldsymbol{x}) = 2/(1-\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^2)$ already takes the amplified value $\lambda(1/\sqrt{2})^2 = 4\,\lambda(\mathbf{0})^2$. At the same time, $1/\sqrt{2}$ stays away from the boundary saturation regime~\citep{guo2022clipped} where $\lambda \to \infty$, gradients vanish, and Poincar\'{e} distances are dominated by the radial coordinate alone. Distances between projected points use the canonical Poincar\'{e} formula
\begin{equation}
d_\mathbb{B}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}) = \mathrm{arcosh}\Big(1 + \frac{2\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}\|^2}{(1-\|\boldsymbol{u}\|^2)(1-\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^2)}\Big).
\end{equation}
The Euclidean and hyperbolic versions of every downstream task cost identical compute on the same features; the only thing that changes between them is the metric.

\subsection{Downstream protocol}
\label{sec:downstream}

For each model and dataset pair, we evaluate two parameter-free, prototype-based metric tasks, each in two versions: Euclidean (R) on the raw feature vectors $\boldsymbol{x}$, and hyperbolic (H) on their post-hoc Poincar\'{e} projection $\phi(\boldsymbol{x})$ from \S\ref{sec:projection}.
\begin{itemize}[topsep=0pt,itemsep=1pt,leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{NC (Nearest-Centroid).} Per-class training mean as the prototype; each test sample is assigned to the class of its nearest prototype. % [COMMITTED to 1Eqj] argmin phrasing fixed
\item \textbf{FS (Few-shot 5-way 5-shot).} $1{,}000$ episodes per model and dataset; per episode $5$ classes, $5$ support + $15$ query samples; classify queries by nearest support-prototype, in the spirit of Prototypical Networks~\citep{snell2017proto}.
\end{itemize}
For each task, we report the hyperbolic advantage
\begin{equation}
H_\text{adv} = (\mathrm{acc}_H - \mathrm{acc}_R) \times 100 \;\; \text{(percentage points)},
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{acc}_R$ denotes the accuracy under raw Euclidean distances and $\mathrm{acc}_H$ the accuracy after applying the post-hoc Poincar\'{e} projection $\phi$ of \S\ref{sec:projection}; both are measured on the same task and split.
Note that $H_{adv}$ is positive when the Poincar\'{e} projection beats the raw Euclidean baseline and negative otherwise. All protocols are training-free: no trainable heads, no learnable distances, no hyperparameter search. Any difference between $R$ and $H$ is attributable to the geometry alone.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Models.} We used 30 foundation models in three paradigms: 14 vision backbones (4 supervised ViTs, 4 self-supervised, 6 contrastive), 9 causal language models, and 7 text-embedding models. % TODO-coauthors: the listed panels below sum to more than 14 vision models; confirm the intended count before submission.
We work with two settings: a main panel of 10 vision backbones evaluated for $\delta$, ORC and two downstream tasks across all 6 datasets (the supervised ViT-T/S/B/L pretrained on ImageNet-21k~\citep{dosovitskiy2021an}, the self-supervised DINOv1-B~\citep{dinov1} and DINOv2-S/B/L/G~\citep{dinov2}, and the contrastive CLIP-B/L~\citep{CLIP}); and a breadth panel of 20 additional models evaluated for cross-modality $\delta$ on ImageNet only (the contrastive variants OpenCLIP~\citep{openclip} and MetaCLIP~\citep{metaclip}; the causal language models GPT-2~\citep{gpt2}, Pythia~\citep{pythia} and OLMo~\citep{olmo}; and the text-embedding families BGE~\citep{BGE}, GTE~\citep{gte} and E5~\citep{e5}).

\paragraph{Datasets and evaluation.} For the cross-model $\delta$ and ORC characterisation (\S\ref{sec:geometry-paradigm}) we use the $1{,}000$ classes of ImageNet~\citep{imagenet} as a shared concept set: vision backbones produce one centroid per class by averaging features of $100$ training images, while causal language models and text-embedding models encode the prompt ``a photo of a \{class\}'' once per class. This common $1{,}000 \times d$ matrix lets us compare $\delta$ and ORC across modalities on equal footing. For the downstream protocol (\S\ref{sec:downstream-results}) we use the $10$ vision backbones of the main panel $\times$ $6$ datasets ($n = 60$): ImageNet~\citep{imagenet}, CIFAR-100~\citep{cifar10}, CIFAR-10~\citep{cifar10}, DTD~\citep{dtd}, FashionMNIST~\citep{fashion}, and MNIST~\citep{mnist}.

% [COMMITTED to 1Eqj] practical protocol moved next to the setup.
\paragraph{Practical protocol.} Compute $\delta$ on training-set class centroids (${\sim}8$~s on CPU). If $\delta < 0.10$ and the labels carry a deep semantic hierarchy, the post-hoc Poincar\'{e} projection of frozen features improves NC and few-shot accuracy by $0.5$--$2.5$~pp; otherwise, retain Euclidean. The decision is reversible (a one-line distance switch), requires no training, and is unconfounded by optimisation tuning, making the geometry a deployable diagnostic rather than only a descriptive one.

\label{sec:geometry-paradigm}

Causal LMs achieve comparably low $\delta$: GPT-2 XL ($0.077$), OLMo-1B ($0.083$), GPT-2 M/L ($0.082$/$0.083$), Pythia-1B ($0.098$). Text embedding models cluster at $\delta \approx 0.110$--$0.124$, identical to vision contrastive models, confirming that the training objective, not the modality, determines the geometry.

\subsection{Downstream task results}
\label{sec:downstream-results}

We evaluate the post-hoc Poincar\'{e} projection of \S\ref{sec:projection} on the two tasks of \S\ref{sec:downstream}, across $10$ vision models $\times$ $6$ datasets ($n = 60$).

\paragraph{Task NC.} The largest gains concentrate where the geometry can be most exploited, the most tree-like backbones on the deepest-hierarchy datasets. DINOv2-L gains $\mathbf{+2.05}$ pp on CIFAR-100 (a $2.4\%$ relative improvement on a $85.6\%$ baseline; McNemar $p \approx 4\times10^{-38}$ on test-set decisions) and $+0.75$ pp on DTD; DINOv2-G gains $+1.23$ pp on CIFAR-100 and DINOv2-S $+0.97$ pp, lower $\delta$ predicts higher $H_{\mathrm{adv}}$ as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:synthesis_pooled}. % [REGEN] audited values; McNemar added (committed to Xbn5)
Within-dataset correlations are strongest on hierarchical datasets, reaching $r = -0.87$ on CIFAR-100 and $r = -0.83$ on ImageNet; flat-hierarchy datasets (FashionMNIST, MNIST) show no significant correlation. % [REGEN]
Outside the SSL family, NC advantages are mostly small or negative: the NC gain is concentrated, not universal. % [REGEN] honest reading of the regenerated NC column
ImageNet NC gains are themselves smaller in absolute terms ($+0.56$ pp for DINOv2-L). % [REGEN]

\paragraph{Task FS.} The few-shot projection helps almost every model and dataset pair ($56/60$ positive $H_{\mathrm{adv}}$, mean $+0.56$ pp), which compresses the variance against which $\delta$ can be tested pooled ($r = -0.18$); within the hierarchical datasets the dependence on $\delta$ is strong: $r = -0.78$ on CIFAR-10, $r = -0.71$ on DTD, $r = -0.67$ on CIFAR-100. % [REGEN] honest pooled value with the compression mechanism stated
All $10$ vision models on ImageNet gain in few-shot ($H_{\mathrm{adv}}$ from $+0.03$ to $+0.91$ pp), with the largest gains again in the SSL family (DINOv2-G $+0.91$, DINOv2-L $+0.89$, DINOv2-B $+0.74$). % [REGEN]
Few-shot gains carry per-episode paired 95\% confidence intervals of $\pm 0.03$--$0.20$ pp across the 60 cells; headline effects exceed their interval by an order of magnitude. % [COMMITTED to Xbn5]

\subsection{Causal ablations: $\delta$ reflects learned hierarchy, not architectural noise}
\label{sec:ablation-noise}

To distinguish architectural bias and label noise from learned hierarchy, we run four interventions on representative vision models. Each isolates a single causal factor as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:causal}.

\textbf{(A1) Random weights.} Random-initialised models have dramatically higher $\delta$: DINOv2-B ($+144\%$), DINOv2-S ($+134\%$), ViT-B ($+65\%$). Tree-like structure is not architectural; it emerges through training.

\textbf{(A2) Non-hierarchical fine-tuning.} Fine-tuning on an 8-class color classification task (no class hierarchy by construction) increases $\delta$: CLIP-B ($+91\%$), DINOv2-S ($+69\%$), ViT-B ($+19\%$). Fine-tuning on hierarchical CIFAR-100 produces minimal change ($-8\%$ to $+6\%$). Same protocol, different task structure, only destroying hierarchy raises $\delta$.

\textbf{(A3) Layer-wise emergence.} Computing $\delta$ on class centroids (1{,}000 ImageNet classes, $50$ imgs/class) at each transformer block, we observe that $\delta$ decreases progressively from layer~1 to the final layer in pretrained transformers: DINOv2-B ($-51\%$, from $0.157$ to $0.077$) and CLIP-B ($-29\%$, from $0.157$ to $0.112$). Hierarchy is not present in shallow layers but is built up across depth, confirming that tree-like structure is a learned property of the representation rather than an artefact of token-level features.

\textbf{(A4) Shuffle control (label noise).} Shuffling embedding-to-label correspondence preserves the point-cloud geometry but destroys semantic association: $\delta$ does not change ($p > 0.12$). This rules out a trivial dependence on high-dimensional point-cloud properties and confirms that $\delta$ measures semantic organisation, not feature norm or covariance.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:reproducibility}

All models and datasets used in this paper are publicly available; the post-hoc Poincar\'{e} projection of \S\ref{sec:projection} is a single \texttt{torch}-level operation determined entirely by training-set statistics ($\boldsymbol\mu$ and $\rho_{95}$). Computing $\delta$ on $1{,}000$ class centroids takes ${\sim}8$~s on CPU, and the full downstream evaluation across the $10$ vision backbones $\times$ $6$ datasets ($n = 60$ combinations) runs in $<\!30$~minutes on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We characterised the geometry of learned class representations and found tree-like inter-class distance structure as a near-universal property of their \emph{form} across modalities and paradigms: read against matched nulls, 69 of 72 vision model$\times$dataset combinations carry genuine tree structure, while the \emph{content} of the tree remains model-specific (the most tree-like family is the least aligned with cross-model agreement). % [COMMITTED] "universal" softened into the form/content statement
Across 30 models, two coordinate-free descriptors ($\delta$ and ORC) converge on the same conclusion. Causal ablations establish that this geometry reflects learned semantic hierarchy: random weights, non-hierarchical fine-tuning, and layer-wise analysis all support the link. The discovery that causal LMs are also tree-like (measured under class-name templates) overturns the assumption that this property is vision-specific. The tree structure is visualised in Figure~\ref{fig:tree_visualization} for DINOv2-S and DINOv2-G on CIFAR-100.

The geometry is not merely descriptive, but prescriptive. Backbone $\delta$ and ORC predict when post-hoc Poincar\'{e} projection improves prototype-based classification (pooled $r = -0.45$ vs $\delta$, $r = +0.43$ vs ORC; up to $r = -0.87$ within CIFAR-100) and few-shot learning ($r = +0.47$ vs ORC; up to $r = -0.78$ within CIFAR-10), and when they do not (datasets without label hierarchy). % [REGEN]
Computing both descriptors takes seconds on CPU, providing a zero-cost diagnostic that tells practitioners whether to use hyperbolic geometry on their specific backbone, dataset combination, and when not to.

The two findings close into a single arc: within hierarchy-respecting paradigms (most strongly self-supervised vision: DINOv2 drops $\delta$ by $33\%$ from S to G while ORC rises from $+0.35$ to $+0.73$, and its null-corrected excess deepens with scale), scaling up the backbone makes the geometry simultaneously more tree-like and more curved, and our correlations show that this is exactly the regime where the post-hoc hyperbolic projection delivers its largest gains (e.g.\ DINOv2-L on CIFAR-100: $H_{\mathrm{adv}}^{\text{NC}} = +2.05$~pp). % [REGEN]
Capacity makes the learned class representation tree-like, and tree-likeness, in turn, makes it exploitable in $\mathbb{H}$.

\textbf{Limitations.} The downstream evaluation covers only two prototype-based tasks, nearest-centroid classification and 5-way 5-shot, and on the headline target $t = 1/\sqrt{2}$ the hyperbolic advantage at any single model and dataset combination is modest in absolute terms ($H_{\mathrm{adv}} \approx 0.5$--$2.1$~pp); whether the same diagnostic predicts gains on retrieval, dense prediction, or end-to-end training in $\mathbb{H}$ is not tested here. % [REGEN] 2.5 -> 2.1 (max regenerated cell)
The Poincar\'{e} ball is fixed at constant negative curvature $-1$ throughout by design, to keep the projection parameter-free, and we do not estimate a curvature value for the representations themselves; our descriptive claims are about tree-like metric structure. % [COMMITTED to RJje]
Causal-LM geometry is measured through class-name templates and is template-sensitive, so those claims are weaker than the vision and embedder results. % [COMMITTED to Xbn5/RJje]
